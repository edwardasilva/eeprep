On a resume, more employers look for teamwork, problem-solving, and communication (often called soft skills) than for technical skills, and they want proof, not a list. That proof is a resume bullet with your part and a number in it, a story you can tell in an interview, or an email that gets answered.

## TLDR;

- Run every project with a written scope, an owner and date for each task, a priced bill of materials, and a weekly status note.
- Show each soft skill in a resume bullet with your part and a number in it, not in the Skills lines.
- On a new-grad resume, put engineering skills first, and add project management and leadership only if there is room left.
- Prepare 5 to 10 interview stories that show problem-solving, teamwork, and communication, the skills employers ask about most, and think out loud in a technical interview.
- Keep each email short, with one action and a deadline, and send a thank-you within 24 hours of a job interview.

## What employers screen for

In the Job Outlook 2026 Spring Update from the National Association of Colleges and Employers (NACE), [85 percent of employers looked on resumes for the ability to work in a team](https://www.naceweb.org/talent-acquisition/candidate-selection/the-key-skills-employers-seek-on-college-students-resumes). Problem-solving came in at 82 percent and verbal communication at 78 percent, ahead of technical skills at 73 percent. The same article says a list is not enough, and that employers want to see examples.

Students rate themselves higher than employers rate them. When NACE compared its 2024 student survey with its Job Outlook 2025 employer survey, [78.1 percent of students rated their own communication 4 or 5 on a five-point proficiency scale](https://www.naceweb.org/career-readiness/competencies/the-gap-in-perceptions-of-new-grads-competency-proficiency-and-resources-to-shrink-it). Of employers, 53.5 percent gave new graduates the same rating. Professionalism showed the same gap, at 79.7 percent against 50.3 percent. Teamwork nearly matched, at 83.5 and 81.5 percent.

You already have evidence from class. ABET, the body that accredits engineering programs, [requires every accredited engineering program to teach students to communicate effectively with a range of audiences](https://www.abet.org/accreditation/accreditation-criteria/criteria-for-accrediting-engineering-programs-2026-2027/). Each program must also teach students to work on a team that sets goals, plans tasks, and meets objectives. Your lab reports, design reviews, and team projects are where that happened.

| Skill              | Where it shows                                         | What the reader looks for                         |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Teamwork           | A project or design-team bullet, and interview stories | The team size and the part that was yours         |
| Problem-solving    | Every project bullet                                   | The fault, how you found it, and the fix          |
| Communication      | Emails, reports, design reviews, and the interview     | Short, clear, and on time                         |
| Professionalism    | Every contact with a recruiter or manager              | Prepared, on time, and following through          |
| Project management | A project you led, or a design-team role               | The scope, the schedule, the budget, what slipped |

## Show it, never list it

The [[Resume#Skills|skills]] section on the resume page leaves soft skills out of the Skills lines, so they go in the bullets. [Michigan Engineering says not to force soft skills into a resume by merely listing them](https://career.engin.umich.edu/resumes-cvs-cover-letters/), and to convey them through achievements. [The University of Virginia's engineering resume guide asks who you did each thing with](https://engineering.virginia.edu/offices-programs/center-engineering-career-development/students/resumes-and-cover-letters), and its example bullet names four teammates and the prototype they built.

A bullet shows teamwork when it names the team and your part in it. "Strong team player" says nothing. "Wired and tested the [X]-cell battery pack for a [N]-person CubeSat power team" names the team, the job, and something an interviewer can ask about.

In the interview, the same skills come back as behavioral questions, which ask how you handled a real situation. [Michigan Engineering says the competencies employers ask about most are problem-solving, teamwork, and communication](https://career.engin.umich.edu/interviews/). It suggests preparing 5 to 10 stories on themes such as conflict, leadership, overcoming challenges, and time management. The [[How to interview#Behavioral stories|behavioral stories]] section on the how to interview page shows how to tell one.

## Run a student project like a real one

Project management means keeping the scope, the schedule, the budget, and the risks in view, and telling people where things stand. Senior design, also called the capstone, is often where electrical engineering students first do it. At the University of Kentucky, [the electrical and computer engineering capstone puts teams of 4 to 6 students on a 30-week project, with about 275 hours of work from each student](https://ece.engr.uky.edu/senior-capstone-design). Each team presents at a requirements review, a preliminary design review, a critical design review, and a final review. Sponsors fund materials budgets from \$1,000 to \$5,000.

Run any project that way, even a small one:

1. Write the scope in two or three sentences: what the build must do, and how you will measure it.
2. Break the work into tasks, each with one owner and a date. A shared spreadsheet or a Gantt chart (a bar chart of tasks against dates) is enough.
3. Keep a bill of materials with prices, and track the spending against the budget.
4. List the risks, such as a part with a long lead time or a footprint nobody has checked, and decide what you will do if each one happens.
5. Send a short status note every week: what got done, what is next, and what is blocked.

On a resume, project management shows up as a bullet with the team size, the budget or the schedule, and your part. One such bullet reads "Kept a [N]-person capstone team's \$[X] parts budget within [X]% across [X] design reviews". The [[Hardware#Teams and undergraduate research|section on teams and undergraduate research]] on the hardware page covers design-team roles that give you this kind of line.

Keep project management in proportion on a new-grad resume. [The r/EngineeringResumes wiki tells a student who is just graduating to show fundamental engineering skills first](https://www.reddit.com/r/EngineeringResumes/wiki/index/), and to add project management and leadership only if there is room left. Its reason is that employers hire a new graduate to master basic engineering tasks first, so the circuit and the code come before the schedule.

## Write so people act

Keep an email short, and make the action plain. [MIT EECS's Communication Lab says to be brief, and to state one action per email where possible](https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/eecs/commkit/email/). Put the topic and the action in the subject line, as in its example "EG Diamond: Characterization needed by Friday", and give a deadline in the body. The same page says to be more formal when in doubt. [Michigan Engineering says to answer an interview invitation within one business day](https://career.engin.umich.edu/interviews/), and to send a thank-you email within 24 hours of the interview.

Write a report so the numbers can be checked. [Illinois's lab report guide for ECE 431 says all data and calculations need relevant units](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/ece431/sp2021/lab/ECE431_lab_report_guide.pdf). When you compare a result to theory, the guide asks for a number such as a percent error, not a vague word about how close it came. It also asks you to number every equation. That is the same habit that puts a measured number in a resume bullet.

Write documentation for someone who cannot ask you. [An IEEE-USA column by a retired electrical engineer says a design document must stand alone and speak for itself](https://insight.ieeeusa.org/articles/effective-communication-skills-vital-for-new-and-emerging-leaders/), because its later readers will not know what the author was thinking. A project README works the same way, and the [[Software#Show it on GitHub|section on showing it on GitHub]] on the software page covers what goes in one. [Michigan Technological University's co-op guide says to document your work when an internship ends, so the next person can refer to your notes](https://www.mtu.edu/career/students/jobs-intern/coop-intern/co-ops/forms/co-op-handbook.pdf).

## Speak so people follow

A design review is for finding problems while there is time to fix them. [Northwestern's design text says the presenter's goal is not to persuade the reviewers that the design is wonderful](https://nuwrite.northwestern.edu/communities/design/docs/engineering-design-and-communication-principles-and-practice/chapter_09.pdf). It tells presenters to respond without getting defensive, to have at least one person take notes, and to sort the comments afterward and decide what to act on.

Have a short version of every project ready. [MIT EECS's Communication Lab suggests a pitch of 20 to 40 seconds](https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/eecs/commkit/elevator-pitch/): the problem, who it affects, what you are doing about it, and how it could help. With a recruiter, lead with why the work matters, not the method, and cut the jargon. At a career fair, that is the version you give first.

In a technical interview, talk while you work. [Michigan Engineering tells students to think out loud](https://career.engin.umich.edu/interviews/), so the interviewer hears how you reason and can steer you when you go wrong.

## A job outside engineering

Keep it on the resume. [An engineering department manager told the magazine of the American Society of Mechanical Engineers](https://magazine.asme.org/issues/january-2026/career-focus-building-a-competitive-resume-that-gets-noticed) that listing a warehouse or restaurant job shows "they weren't sitting around doing nothing", and that it shows character. Give it one or two bullets until you have an internship or other technical work, and after that, keep it as one line, as the [[Resume#Experience|experience]] section on the resume page says.

Skip the duty every reader already knows, and count something instead. [Colorado State's career center lists what to count in a service job](https://career.colostate.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Translating-Serivce-Industry-Experience-on-Your-Resume-Fall-2023.pdf): customers handled a day, trainees, error-free transactions a shift, and compliance with health and safety standards. [Michigan Engineering rewrites "Assisted customers with merchandise questions"](https://career.engin.umich.edu/resumes-cvs-cover-letters/) as a bullet about studying the store layout to answer the questions customers asked most. The bullet for Alex's sales job reads "Processed about [X] sales and returns a shift at the register". Fill each [X] with a number you can defend.

## Habits an intern is judged on

[Michigan Tech's co-op guide expects students to be punctual and present every day of work](https://www.mtu.edu/career/students/jobs-intern/coop-intern/co-ops/forms/co-op-handbook.pdf), and to get an absence approved in advance. Its other rules hold for any internship:

1. Ask questions and take notes from the first day.
2. Try a coworker, the documentation, or a search first. Then ask your supervisor, and write the answer down, because the guide says to avoid asking the same question again.
3. If you will miss a deadline, say so early, so nobody counts on work that is not coming.
4. Take initiative. In a slow week, ask what you can help with.
5. Before you leave, write down where your work stands.

[NACE's career readiness competencies list "be present and prepared" as one sign of professionalism](https://www.naceweb.org/career-readiness/competencies/career-readiness-defined), and its communication competency asks you to tell others promptly when you need guidance on a task.
