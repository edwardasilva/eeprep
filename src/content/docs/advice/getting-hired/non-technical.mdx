Show a non-technical skill with something you did, and never just list it. Employers screen for teamwork, problem-solving, and communication about as hard as for technical skills, and they want proof. That proof is a bullet with your part and a number in it, a story you can tell in an interview, or an email that gets answered.

## What employers screen for

[In NACE's Job Outlook 2026 Spring Update, 85 percent of employers looked for the ability to work in a team on a resume](https://www.naceweb.org/talent-acquisition/candidate-selection/the-key-skills-employers-seek-on-college-students-resumes). Problem-solving came in at 82 percent and verbal communication at 78 percent, ahead of technical skills at 73 percent. The same article says a list is not enough, and that employers want to see examples.

Students rate themselves higher than employers rate them. [In NACE's most recent comparison, 78.1 percent of students rated their own communication very or extremely proficient, against 53.5 percent of employers rating new graduates](https://www.naceweb.org/career-readiness/competencies/the-gap-in-perceptions-of-new-grads-competency-proficiency-and-resources-to-shrink-it). Professionalism showed the same gap, at 79.7 percent against 50.3 percent. Teamwork nearly matched, at 83.5 and 81.5 percent.

You already have evidence from class. [ABET requires every accredited engineering program to teach students to communicate effectively with a range of audiences](https://www.abet.org/accreditation/accreditation-criteria/criteria-for-accrediting-engineering-programs-2026-2027/), and to work on a team that sets goals, plans tasks, and meets objectives. Your lab reports, design reviews, and team projects are where that happened.

| Skill              | Where it shows                                         | What the reader looks for                         |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Teamwork           | A project or design-team bullet, and interview stories | The team size and the part that was yours         |
| Problem-solving    | Every project bullet                                   | The fault, how you found it, and the fix          |
| Communication      | Emails, reports, design reviews, and the interview     | Short, clear, and on time                         |
| Professionalism    | Every contact with a recruiter or manager              | Prepared, on time, and following through          |
| Project management | A project you led, or a design-team role               | The scope, the schedule, the budget, what slipped |

## Show it, never list it

The [[Resume#Skills|skills]] section on the resume page cuts soft skills from the Skills lines, so they go in the bullets. [Michigan Engineering says not to force soft skills into a resume by merely listing them](https://career.engin.umich.edu/resumes-cvs-cover-letters/), and to convey them through achievements. [UVA Engineering's resume guide asks who you did each thing with](https://engineering.virginia.edu/offices-programs/center-engineering-career-development/students/resumes-and-cover-letters), and its example bullet names four teammates and the prototype they built.

A bullet shows teamwork when it names the team and your part in it. "Strong team player" says nothing. "Wired and tested the [X]-cell battery pack for a [N]-person CubeSat power team" names the team, the job, and something an interviewer can ask about.

In the interview, the same skills come back as behavioral questions. [Michigan Engineering says the competencies employers ask about most are problem solving, teamwork, and communication](https://career.engin.umich.edu/interviews/). It suggests preparing 5 to 10 stories on themes such as conflict, leadership, failure, and time management. The [[How to interview#Behavioral stories|behavioral stories]] section on the how to interview page shows how to tell one.

## Run a student project like a real one

Project management means keeping the scope, the schedule, the budget, and the risks in view, and telling people where things stand. [ABET's teamwork outcome asks students to establish goals, plan tasks, and meet objectives](https://www.abet.org/accreditation/accreditation-criteria/criteria-for-accrediting-engineering-programs-2026-2027/). Senior design is where most EE students do it for the first time. [Kentucky's ECE capstone puts teams of 4 to 6 students on a 30-week project, with about 275 hours of work from each student](https://ece.engr.uky.edu/senior-capstone-design). The team presents at a requirements review, a preliminary design review, a critical design review, and a final review. Sponsors fund materials budgets from \$1,000 to \$5,000.

Run any project that way, even a small one:

1. Write the scope in two or three sentences: what the build must do, and how you will measure it.
2. Break the work into tasks, each with one owner and a date. A shared spreadsheet or a Gantt chart is enough.
3. Keep a bill of materials with prices, and track the spending against the budget.
4. List the risks, such as a part with a long lead time or a footprint nobody has checked, and decide what you will do if each one happens.
5. Send a short status note every week: what got done, what is next, and what is blocked.

On a resume, project management shows up as a bullet with the team size, the budget or the schedule, and your part, such as "Kept a [N]-person capstone team's \$[X] parts budget within [X]% across [X] design reviews". The [[Hardware#Teams and undergraduate research|section on teams and undergraduate research]] on the hardware page covers design-team roles that give you this kind of line. Keep it in proportion. [The wiki tells a student who is just graduating to show fundamental engineering skills first](https://www.reddit.com/r/EngineeringResumes/wiki/index/), and to add project management and leadership only if there is room left. Its reason is that employers hire a new graduate to master basic engineering tasks first, so the circuit and the code come before the schedule.

## Write so people act

Keep an email short, and make the action plain. [MIT EECS's Communication Lab says to be brief, and to state one action per email where possible](https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/eecs/commkit/email/). Put the topic and the action in the subject line, as in its example "EG Diamond: Characterization needed by Friday", and give a deadline in the body. The same page says to be more formal when in doubt. [Michigan Engineering says to answer an interview invitation within one business day](https://career.engin.umich.edu/interviews/), and to send a thank-you email within 24 hours of the interview.

Write a report so the numbers can be checked. [Illinois's lab report guide for ECE 431 says all data and calculations need relevant units](https://courses.grainger.illinois.edu/ece431/sp2021/lab/ECE431_lab_report_guide.pdf). It asks for a percent error when you compare a result to theory, never "very close", and it numbers every equation. That is the same habit that puts a measured number in a resume bullet.

Write documentation for someone who cannot ask you. [An IEEE-USA column by a retired electrical engineer says a design document must stand alone and speak for itself](https://insight.ieeeusa.org/articles/effective-communication-skills-vital-for-new-and-emerging-leaders/), because its later readers will not know what the author was thinking. A project README works the same way, and the [[Software#Show it on GitHub|section on showing it on GitHub]] on the software page covers what goes in one. When an internship ends, [Michigan Tech's co-op guide says to document your work so the next person can refer to your notes](https://www.mtu.edu/career/students/jobs-intern/coop-intern/co-ops/forms/co-op-handbook.pdf).

## Speak so people follow

A design review is for finding problems while there is time to fix them. [Northwestern's design text says the presenter's goal is not to persuade the reviewers that the design is wonderful](https://nuwrite.northwestern.edu/communities/design/docs/engineering-design-and-communication-principles-and-practice/chapter_09.pdf). It tells presenters to respond without getting defensive, to have at least one person take notes, and to sort the comments afterward and decide what to act on.

Have a short version of every project ready. [MIT EECS's Communication Lab suggests a pitch of 20 to 40 seconds](https://mitcommlab.mit.edu/eecs/commkit/elevator-pitch/): the problem, who it affects, what you are doing about it, and how it could help. With a recruiter, lead with why the work matters, not the method, and cut the jargon. At a career fair, that is the version you give first.

In a technical interview, talk while you work. [Michigan Engineering tells students to think out loud](https://career.engin.umich.edu/interviews/), so the interviewer hears how you reason and can steer you when you go wrong.

## A job outside engineering

Keep it on the resume. [An engineering department manager told ASME's magazine](https://magazine.asme.org/issues/january-2026/career-focus-building-a-competitive-resume-that-gets-noticed) that listing a warehouse or restaurant job shows "they weren't sitting around doing nothing", and that it shows character. The [[Resume#Experience|experience]] section on the resume page says when the job gets bullets. Until you have an engineering job, it does.

Skip the duty every reader already knows, and count something instead. [Colorado State's career center lists what to count in a service job](https://career.colostate.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/Translating-Serivce-Industry-Experience-on-Your-Resume-Fall-2023.pdf): customers handled a day, trainees, error-free transactions a shift, and compliance with health and safety standards. [Michigan Engineering rewrites "Assisted customers with merchandise questions"](https://career.engin.umich.edu/resumes-cvs-cover-letters/) as a bullet about studying the store layout to answer the questions customers asked most. Alex's sales job reads "Processed about [X] sales and returns a shift at the register". Every [X] is a number you can defend.

## Habits an intern is judged on

[Michigan Tech's co-op guide expects students to be punctual and present every day of work](https://www.mtu.edu/career/students/jobs-intern/coop-intern/co-ops/forms/co-op-handbook.pdf), and to get an absence approved in advance. Its other rules hold for any internship:

1. Ask questions and take notes from the first day.
2. Try a co-worker, the documentation, or a search first. Then ask, and write the answer down, because the guide says to avoid asking the same question again.
3. If you will miss a deadline, say so early, so nobody counts on work that is not coming.
4. Take initiative. In a slow week, ask what you can help with.
5. Before you leave, write down where your work stands.

[NACE's career readiness competencies put professionalism in four words](https://www.naceweb.org/career-readiness/competencies/career-readiness-defined): be present and prepared. The same list asks you to tell others promptly when you need guidance on a task.
