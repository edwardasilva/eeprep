Talk to people before you need them. The contacts you make now turn into referrals later, and a referral puts your resume in front of a person instead of a filter.

## TLDR;

- Ask people who already know you or your work for a referral first, and find them with the list on the [[Getting hired#Get a referral first|getting hired page]].
- Then find alumni through your school's alumni tab on LinkedIn, and ask each one for 20 minutes about their work, not for a job.
- Ask an alumnus for a referral only once they know what you built, and send the job title, job ID, link, and your resume.
- If a referral request gets no answer, follow up once after a week, and apply on your own within two weeks of the posting going up.
- Go to the career fair with one project ready to show, and say what you measured.

## Why a referral is worth the effort

Economists at the New York Fed matched applications to outcomes at one large US firm. [Referred people were 6 percent of the applicants, 21 percent of the interviewees, 27 percent of the offer recipients, and 29 percent of the hires](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/staff_reports/sr568). The referral funnel figure on this page shows the four numbers.

<ReferralFunnelFigure />

Lever, a company that sells hiring software, found the same pattern. [Lever's 2016 benchmarks put the odds of a hire at one in 16 for a referred candidate and one in 152 for someone who applied through the career site](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2016/10/05/877340/0/en/New-Research-Shows-Only-One-in-100-Candidates-is-Hired-Referred-Candidates-Nearly-10-Times-as-Likely-to-be-Hired-as-Applicants.html).

[Ashby's report on 38 million applications from 2021 to 2024](https://www.ashbyhq.com/talent-trends-report/reports/referrals), from another hiring software company, found that referrals fell from 2 percent of applications in early 2021 to under 1 percent three years later. Over the same years, the offer rate for referred candidates fell from 12.1 percent to 7.3 percent. The rate for people who applied on their own fell faster, from 7 in 1,000 to 2 in 1,000, so in relative terms a referral's edge over a cold application has grown.

A referred resume goes to a recruiter, and a cold one goes into the pile, even when the two candidates did the same work. [An IEEE Spectrum careers column says a referral from a trusted connection lifts you above a pile of AI-filled applications](https://spectrum.ieee.org/improving-interview-odds).

The [[Getting hired#Get a referral first|section on getting a referral first]] on the getting hired page has the list of people to ask and the message to send.

## The informational interview

An informational interview is a short, informal conversation about someone's work, usually 20 to 30 minutes long. When you ask for one, mention how you got the person's name and make clear you want information, not a job. [Sacramento State's tip sheet](https://www.csus.edu/college/business-administration/graduate/_internal/_docs/informational-interview_tips-2.pdf) gives that length and that advice. [Georgia Tech's handout](https://www.scheller.gatech.edu/_files/pdf-pages/undergraduate/Informational-Interview.pdf) says plainly that an informational interview is never used to ask for a job.

A good request has five parts:

1. How you found them. A shared class, team, school, or a mutual contact.
2. One line on who you are and what you build.
3. A specific ask: 20 minutes, by phone or video, in the next two weeks.
4. The words "advice" or "information", and nothing about a job.
5. A subject line that says what the message is, not "Introduction".

Copy this for an email, or for a LinkedIn message once the person accepts your connection request, and fill in the brackets. A connection note holds only 200 characters, so the LinkedIn section below has a short version.

```text wrap
Subject: [School] EE student, 20 minutes on test engineering at [Company]?

Hi [Name],

I found you through the [School] alumni page. I am a fourth-year electrical engineering student, and I am building a [one-line project].

Could I ask you 20 minutes of questions about test engineering at [Company], sometime in the next two weeks? I want to understand the work, not ask for a job.

Thanks either way.

Alex Doe
```

Prepare five questions about the work. Ask what a normal week looks like, what the team wishes new hires knew, and what they would build if they were a student now. [MIT's career office lists more suggested questions for informational interviews](https://capd.mit.edu/resources/suggested-questions-for-informational-interviewing/).

During the conversation, write down one thing to follow up on. Send a thank-you note the same day. [The National Institutes of Health (NIH) career office says to send one promptly](https://www.training.nih.gov/documents/25/2024_Informational_Interview_Handout.pdf), and [MIT's career office sets a limit of 24 hours](https://capd.mit.edu/resources/conducting-informational-interviews/).

## Asking for the referral

Ask after the conversation, not in it, and only once the person has seen your work. A referrer puts their own name next to yours, so make it easy for them. Say how you know each other, and send the job title, the job ID, the link, and your resume. Give them an easy way to say no. The [[Getting hired#Get a referral first|section on getting a referral first]] on the getting hired page has the script.

Do not open with a referral request. Blind is an anonymous forum for tech workers. [An engineering manager there wrote that introducing yourself, describing your situation, and thanking the person set you far apart](https://www.teamblind.com/post/iMeq6kEY) from people who only write "can I have one". The same manager asks for a link to your resume in the first message.

Most requests do not get a referral. [A study of referral requests posted on Blind in 2024 found that 54 percent got no referral offer, and 31 percent got no comments at all](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.10726). Send one follow-up after a week, then move to the next person. If the posting has been up for almost two weeks, apply on your own instead of waiting, as the [[Getting hired#Get a referral first|getting hired page]] says.

## Career fairs

In the [2024 student survey by the National Association of Colleges and Employers (NACE)](https://naceweb.org/talent-acquisition/student-attitudes/more-than-half-of-students-attended-a-career-fair-in-the-past-12-months), more than half of students had attended a career fair in the past 12 months. Of those, more than 45 percent got an interview offer after the fair, and nearly one quarter got a job offer. Employers agree that fairs work. [In NACE's 2025 internship report, more than two thirds of employers rated in-person fairs and on-campus recruiting effective for 2023–24](https://naceweb.org/talent-acquisition/internships/in-person-activities-more-effective-than-virtual-ones-for-recruiting-interns).

The odds at one booth are lower than those numbers sound. [An engineering manager in the power industry wrote that they took in around 75 resumes for one internship](https://www.reddit.com/r/EngineeringStudents/comments/1rrudb3/), and the students who got picked had a conversation the manager remembered. The [[Getting hired#Cold outreach and career fairs|cold outreach and career fairs]] section on the getting hired page has the four-line card to write before you go.

The big society conventions are career fairs too. [The Society of Women Engineers' WE25 fair had hundreds of organizations on the floor](https://we25.swe.org/schedule-highlight/career-fair/). [The Department of Energy expected 400-plus career fair exhibitors at the 2025 convention of the National Society of Black Engineers](https://www.energy.gov/node/4848689). The [[Professional societies|professional societies page]] covers which ones to join.

The recruiter at the booth is paid to evaluate you, and the conversation is the interview. Have one project ready to show, say what you measured, and ask what their team is hiring for this year.

## LinkedIn

Use LinkedIn to find people, not to apply. Open your school's LinkedIn page and use the alumni tab. [Santa Clara's career center explains its filters](https://www.scu.edu/careercenter/toolkit/networking/alumnitool/), which sort alumni by where they work, what they do, and what they studied. Search for the company, then for your major.

[Free accounts get three personalized connection notes a month, each up to 200 characters](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a563153). Spend them on alumni and second-degree contacts (people your connections know), and use the note to ask for an informational interview. Here is an example note. It is 182 characters as written, so keep what you put in the brackets short.

```text wrap
Hi [Name], I am a [School] EE student building a [one-line project]. Could I ask you 20 minutes of questions about your work at [Company]? I am not asking for a job. Thanks, Alex Doe
```

The [[Portfolio|portfolio page]] covers the profile itself, and its [[Portfolio#Post the work as you go|section on posting the work as you go]] covers the posts.

[MIT's career office has a five-step guide to the profile](https://capd.mit.edu/resources/linkedin-2/), and [Georgia Tech's networking and virtual identity guide covers outreach messages and what to do when nobody answers](https://sites.gatech.edu/careercenterdev/files/2024/09/Networking-and-Online-Persona_Career-Guide.pdf).

Post what you are building. A photo of the bench and one sentence on the result is enough. The alumni you message can see the work before they answer, and a recruiter who searches for your tools finds proof instead of a headline.
