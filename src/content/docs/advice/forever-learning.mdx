Read the datasheet, the app note, and the errata for every part you use. Fill gaps with free university courses, and join your IEEE student branch. Take the FE exam near graduation if you might want a PE license. Once you work, ask whether your employer pays for classes. Keep building, and write down what you learn.

Alex Doe from [[Resume]] and [[Projects]] is the running example.

## Read the part's documents

The datasheet is where you start, but it is not the only document for a part. Vendors also publish application notes and errata.

An application note shows how to use a part in a circuit. Alex's buck converter is a good match for [TI's app note on buck converter power stage math](https://www.ti.com/lit/an/slva477c/slva477c.pdf). It gives the formulas for a buck converter with an integrated switch in continuous conduction mode. Work those formulas by hand before you trust a simulation.

An errata sheet lists where the silicon does not match the datasheet. [The MSP430F5529 errata sheet](https://www.ti.com/lit/er/slaz314/slaz314.pdf) describes the chip's known exceptions to its functional specifications, one advisory at a time. Check the errata before you debug firmware that looks right and still fails. Match the advisories to your chip's revision marking.

## Take free courses

[MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/about/) publishes materials from more than 2,500 MIT courses for free. Three of them cover the core of an EE degree:

- [6.002 Circuits and Electronics](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-002-circuits-and-electronics-spring-2007/) is MIT's first course for EE and EECS students.
- [6.003 Signals and Systems](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-003-signals-and-systems-fall-2011/) covers Fourier, Laplace, and Z transforms, sampling, and linear time-invariant systems.
- [6.004 Computation Structures](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-004-computation-structures-spring-2017/) runs from CMOS gates to a pipelined processor and interrupts.

Pick the course behind the part of your job or project you understand least. Work the problem sets as well as the lectures.

## Join IEEE

IEEE offers [student membership](https://www.ieee.org/membership/join) and supports student branches at many schools. If your school has a branch, go to its meetings and talks.

## The FE exam and the PE license

A professional engineer (PE) license lets you sign and seal engineering drawings. [NCEES says only a licensed engineer can prepare, sign, seal, and submit them](https://ncees.org/licensure/).

[NCEES lists the usual path](https://ncees.org/licensure/) in three steps:

1. A bachelor's degree from an ABET-accredited engineering program.
2. About four years of work experience, as most states require.
3. Passing the Fundamentals of Engineering (FE) exam and then the PE exam.

Take the FE early. [NCEES designs the FE exam](https://ncees.org/exams/fe-exam/) for recent graduates and for students close to finishing an ABET-accredited degree. It is a computer-based exam of 110 questions, offered year-round at Pearson test centers, with a \$225 fee.

The PE comes after the work experience. [The PE Electrical and Computer exam](https://ncees.org/exams/pe-exam/electrical-and-computer/) comes in three versions: Computer Engineering, Electrical and Electronics, and Power. Your state board sets the final rules, so check its requirements before you register.

## Employer tuition help

Ask about tuition help before you accept an offer, and again once you start. [IRS Publication 15-B](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p15b) lets an employer pay up to \$5,250 a year of an employee's education costs as a tax-free benefit.

## Keep building and keep notes

A project keeps teaching after the class ends. Build one that pushes on what you learned least in school. [[Projects]] covers how to pick one, and [[Software#Show it on GitHub]] covers the write-up.

Keep a notebook for every build. Write down the part number, the measurement, the instrument, and what broke. Alex writes the date and the board revision on every page, so each number traces back to a test. Those notes become your resume lines and your interview answers. [[How to interview#Your projects, line by line]] lists the questions they answer.

## Checklist

- Download the datasheet, app notes, and errata for each part on your next board.
- Work one problem set from [6.002](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-002-circuits-and-electronics-spring-2007/) or [6.003](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-003-signals-and-systems-fall-2011/).
- Go to one [IEEE student branch](https://www.ieee.org/membership/join) meeting this term.
- If you might want a PE, take the [FE exam](https://ncees.org/exams/fe-exam/) before you graduate.
- Ask your employer about [tuition help](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p15b).
- Keep a dated notebook for every build.
