I'm an electrical engineer, and I write embedded software for a large aerospace hardware company. Several rules on the other pages come from the path below.

## Background

I picked electrical engineering because it seemed the most math-intensive subject that still let me move back and forth between physical engineering and computer science. I loved the math. I graduated in May 2026 with a BS in electrical engineering and a minor in computer science. The minor put me through embedded systems, computer organization, and digital signal processing, alongside the circuits and controls courses.

Before any engineering job, I worked customer service, taught coding, drove for two florists, and graded for calculus courses. Those jobs gave me the ability to talk to anyone about anything, at any level. I learned what it means to communicate and build relationships, and how not to be the stereotypical engineer who can't talk. When you list jobs like these, talk about the soft skills they gave you. [[Resume#Experience]] covers the format.

## First engineering work

In fall 2024 I joined a faculty-led lab as an undergraduate researcher. I reviewed the literature on sensor systems and farm-level control for offshore wind, and wrote it up in LaTeX reports.

I found my next two roles through LinkedIn and my school's internal job board. From January to June 2025 I did an electrical design co-op at an engineering firm in Denver. I worked on multi-unit residential buildings: load and voltage-drop calculations, panel schedules, riser diagrams, and National Electrical Code checks. The co-op taught me that I wanted something more cerebral, with more depth, closer to study and academia.

That summer I interned at a defense contractor on real-time signal processing code in C++. I got a 1.6x speedup out of a legacy signal chain with AVX-512 vectorization, and I built the team a logging framework. The internship showed me that I love hard problems, and DSP has some of the hardest. Embedded systems came along as a side effect. I learned I was good at programming, but I wanted work that still used the physical engineering from my EE degree.

An unfinished project helped me get that internship. I built a solar tracker in July 2022 that crudely followed the light every 2 minutes. In August 2024 I restarted it to make the tracking smooth. The restart reached a working sensor prototype, but the motors and housing never got built. It still showed I could build things on my own, so I shared my progress with the company. [[Projects]] covers how to pick and present a project.

## Senior year

Senior year ran on controls and signal processing, and I found I loved both. My resume had a 50 Hz gantry crane controller, EKF and UKF state estimation, and a real-time pitch tracker in MATLAB. My capstone was a bicycle damper, and I owned its electromagnet design.

My courses showed up in those projects. I modeled the damper's electromagnet with the Biot-Savart law from electromagnetics, and its flux path used the magnetic circuits from electric machinery. The internship showed up too. On a maze robot, our first I2C packets sent each value as an 8-byte double, but the Arduino only had room for a 4-byte float. I only knew how to debug that because the internship's SIMD work taught me a lot about registers and floating-point conversions.

On the state estimation project, I tuned the filters for the simulation and simple inputs. If I did it again, I'd add more randomness to those inputs and tune against it.

## The job hunt

I sent hundreds of applications and tried everything to see what would stick. I found most openings on LinkedIn. I also wrote a web scraper with Perplexity and Claude that told me whenever a company I followed opened a new role. I applied to each opening by hand, on the company's own site. Use the tools you have to get the work done.

I couldn't get a referral at my current employer, so I applied cold, and no one inside vouched for me. My scraper flagged the opening, and I applied the day it posted. My advice is to be scrappy, especially at big companies. [[Getting hired#Get a referral first]] covers the referral side.

## What didn't work

My rule is 20 applications. If 20 bring zero interviews, stop and change the resume or the channel before you send 20 more. I learned it by feel, not from data. It was trial and error, and when the failures kept coming, the problem was the method I was using.

My early resumes weren't formatted properly, lacked the language and skills these pages cover, and didn't use a proper template. Then I found [the LaTeX template on r/EngineeringResumes](https://www.reddit.com/r/EngineeringResumes/wiki/templates/), and everything changed. It's the one I recommend. [[Resume]] has those fixes, and [[Getting hired#When online applications go quiet]] puts the 20-application rule to work.

## Referrals

I learned from the other side that referrals work. About three months after I started, once people knew my name, I referred several friends. Each one went straight to the top of the resume pile. In my experience, a referral carries weight once the person giving it is known on the team. One would have made my own process far easier.

## Interviews

The process was long, tiring, and hard. A recruiter first pitched me for a power electronics role, and the hiring manager moved me to software. [[How to interview#The recruiter moved the file]] tells that part. Then came a second phone screen, a three-hour take-home coding challenge, and a call with that manager.

The on-site day opened with a factory tour that counted as part of the interview. Engineers pointed at equipment and asked how I thought it worked. Then I gave an hour-long talk on my internship work to six engineers, most of whom are now on my team. They interrupted constantly to push on the details.

Four one-hour interviews followed, mostly at a whiteboard: system design, my capstone in depth, a coding problem, and a behavioral talk with my manager. One whiteboard session asked how to build a computer from the ground up, and none asked me to debug. Lunch counted too, and the day ran 6 to 7 hours.

At the end of each session, I tried to ask something that challenged the interviewer the way they had challenged me. I wanted to make them think, and to put some pressure on them, personally and professionally. Next time I'd come with more research on the company, and I'd get the recruiters to brag, because everyone loves to brag.

Within half an hour of the last interview, I knew I was moving on. Half-hour calls with a director and then a vice president closed it out. [[How to interview#The talk and the board]] has more on the day, and [[How to interview]] walks through how to prepare.

What I took from it is to be confident without being arrogant, and to never bluff. Know everything you talk about in as much detail as you can. Explaining and defending my thinking out loud is still most of the job.

## Where I am now

I started in July 2026 as an embedded software engineer, and I write drivers in C and C++. The company's mission sold me, and I love working there. I love RF even more than DSP and controls, because of its heavy math and science.

I started eeprep because people need help with their resumes. I built a system that worked, and eeprep is where I write it down.

## What I'd tell a student now

Above everything else, have confidence in yourself, your abilities, and your communication skills. The jobs before engineering gave me the communication part, and the interview day tested all three. Take extreme ownership whenever you can. I owned the electromagnet design on my capstone, so the hour on it at the whiteboard was mine to defend. Give tangible results that anyone can understand the value of. A 1.6x speedup says more to a reader than a list of tasks, and [[Resume#Bullets]] shows how to write one.

The rest, in short:

- Ask for a referral before you apply. [[Getting hired#Get a referral first]] lists who to ask.
- If you can't get one, apply the day the posting opens.
- If 20 applications bring zero interviews, change the resume or the channel.
- Show a project that isn't finished if the progress is real.
- Tie each class to something you built, so you can say where you used it.
- Know every line of your resume in full detail, because interviewers will ask.
- Practice LeetCode-style problems out loud at a whiteboard.
- Never bluff.
