Build one project you can defend. Put it on a one-page resume with a measured number in every bullet you can. Ask for a referral, then apply within two weeks of the posting, and on the first day if you can. After about 50 applications with zero interviews, reevaluate the resume, the channel you apply through, and the roles you target, then keep going. The 50 is a rule of thumb, not a measured cutoff. Know every line of your resume when the interview comes, and never bluff. The rest of this page gives each step a few sentences and names the page that goes deeper.

## 1. Build one project you can defend

Pick one build, aim it at what your target company makes, and finish it with a measured number. Write down what broke and how you fixed it. One project you can explain line by line beats five you copied. [One commenter's projects got them a job at Apple](https://www.reddit.com/r/EngineeringStudents/comments/1odiu6l/personal_projects_that_got_you_hired_for/): an oscilloscope built on an STM32 board with 9 percent error, and a microcontroller built in Vivado that ran a Mario game. The [[Projects|projects page]] covers how to pick and present a build. The [[Software|software page]] covers firmware and lab scripts, and the [[Hardware|hardware page]] covers bench skills and builds that fit a student budget.

## 2. Write one page a screener can read in seconds

One column, no photo, dates at the right margin, and a result in every bullet. [One engineering hiring manager reads about 350 resumes a week](https://www.reddit.com/r/EngineeringResumes/comments/1hw10ms/12_yoe_some_long_direct_advice_in_tech_from_a/) and finds maybe 20 good ones, so the first scan has to land. In [Ashby's 2026 recruiter data](https://www.ashbyhq.com/talent-trends-report/reports/2023-recruiter-productivity-trends-report), the average recruiter handles 291 applications per hire. The [[Resume|resume page]] has the layout and the bullet pattern. The [[Walkthrough|walkthrough page]] takes the resume of Alex Doe, a made-up student, from a first draft to the finished PDF. The [[Cleanup|cleanup page]] is the audit to run before you send it.

## 3. Ask for a referral, then apply early

A referral means someone at the company puts your name forward for a specific job. In [Ashby's report on 38 million applications from 2021 to 2024](https://www.ashbyhq.com/talent-trends-report/reports/referrals), 40 percent of referred candidates got an interview and 3 percent of people who applied on their own did. Ask before you apply, and apply within two weeks of the posting with or without a referral, because [most recruiters review applications in the order they arrive](https://my.greenhouse.com/blogs/what-really-happens-after-you-apply-for-a-job). The [[Getting hired|getting hired page]] lists who to ask and the message to send.

## 4. Reevaluate after 50 applications with zero interviews

After about 50 applications with zero interviews, reevaluate the resume, the channel you apply through, and the roles you target, then keep going. A phone screen counts as an interview, even a short call with a recruiter. Tailoring your resume to every posting is often unrealistic over dozens of applications. eeprep's rule of thumb is to build a master resume and keep [[Resume#Tailor it to the posting|one version for each kind of role]] you apply to, up to 4 or 5. Send the closest one, and tailor by hand only for a posting that matters. To change the channel, talk to a professor who knows engineers, or meet the recruiter at a career fair. One commenter cited on the getting hired page got no interviews from online applications unless they had already met the company at a fair. Those calls came while the recruiter was still on campus. The getting hired page tells the stories behind the rule in its [[Getting hired#When online applications go quiet|section on when online applications go quiet]].

## 5. Know every line of your resume

[A recruiter on X says hiring managers go 3 to 5 layers deep](https://x.com/randomrecruiter/status/2081829972415578607) on whatever you say: how did you do that, why that way, what broke. [Harvard's career center also tells students to be prepared to discuss everything on their resume](https://careerservices.fas.harvard.edu/resources/interviewing/). Prepare your own projects first, then fundamentals, then coding out loud, then a handful of STAR (situation, task, action, result) stories. Say what you do not know, and never bluff. The [[How to interview|how to interview page]] follows a typical process from the phone screen to the offer.

## 6. Match the calendar

Internship postings open before new-grad postings, so apply for internships in late summer and fall. Full-time hiring has two peaks, [from September to November and from January to March](https://ocs.yale.edu/industry-hiring-timelines/). The [[First internship|first internship page]] covers the internship calendar and what to do if a summer internship falls through. The [[First full-time role|first full-time role page]] covers senior-year recruiting, return offers, and reading an offer.

## 7. Keep learning after the offer

Read the datasheet, the application note, and the errata (the list of known problems) for every part you use. Take the Fundamentals of Engineering (FE) exam near graduation if a professional engineer license might ever matter to you. The [[Forever learning|forever learning page]] lists the free courses and the paperwork. The [[AI use|AI use page]] says where an AI tool helps in the job search, such as finding postings, checking a draft, and rehearsing interviews, and where it hurts.

## Where the rule of 50 came from

The rule started as 20, from trial and error in a real search, and the [[Author's story|author's story page]] has the short version. It is 50 now because [3 percent of people who applied on their own got an interview in Ashby's data](https://www.ashbyhq.com/talent-trends-report/reports/referrals). At that rate, a run of 20 applications with zero interviews happens about half the time by luck alone. The job market page has the numbers in its [[Job market#Why reevaluate at 50|section on why to reevaluate at 50]].
