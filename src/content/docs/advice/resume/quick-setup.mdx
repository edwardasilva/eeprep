Build one project you can defend. Put it on a one-page resume with a measured number in every bullet you can. Ask for a referral, then apply within two weeks of the posting, and on the first day if you can. After about 20 applications with no interviews, pause and reevaluate the resume, the channel, and the roles you target, then adjust and keep going. The 20 is a rule of thumb from experience, not a measured cutoff. Know every line of your resume when the interview comes, and never bluff. That is the whole guide. The rest of this page gives each step a few sentences and names the page that goes deeper.

## 1. Build one project you can defend

Pick one build, aim it at what the company makes, and finish it with a measured number. Write down what broke and how you fixed it. One project you can explain line by line beats five you copied. [One student's projects got them a job at Apple](https://www.reddit.com/r/EngineeringStudents/comments/1odiu6l/personal_projects_that_got_you_hired_for/): an oscilloscope built on an STM32 board with 9% error, and an MCU in Vivado that ran a Mario game. The [[Projects|projects page]] covers how to pick and present a build, the [[Software|software page]] covers firmware and lab scripts, and the [[Hardware|hardware page]] covers bench skills and builds on a student budget.

## 2. Write one page a screener can read in seconds

One column, no photo, dates at the right margin, and a result in every bullet. [One engineering hiring manager reads about 350 resumes a week](https://www.reddit.com/r/EngineeringResumes/comments/1hw10ms/12_yoe_some_long_direct_advice_in_tech_from_a/) and finds maybe 20 good ones, so the first scan has to land. The [[Resume|resume page]] has the layout and the bullet pattern. The [[Walkthrough|walkthrough page]] takes Alex's page from a first draft to the finished PDF, and the [[Cleanup|cleanup page]] is the audit to run before you send it.

## 3. Ask for a referral, then apply early

A referral is someone at the company putting your name forward for a specific job. In [Ashby's report on 38 million applications](https://www.ashbyhq.com/talent-trends-report/reports/referrals), 40% of referred candidates got an interview and 3% of people who applied on their own did. Ask before you apply. If you cannot get one, apply within two weeks of the posting, because [most recruiters read applications in the order they arrive](https://my.greenhouse.com/blogs/what-really-happens-after-you-apply-for-a-job). The [[Getting hired|getting hired page]] lists who to ask and the message to send.

## 4. Reevaluate after 20 quiet applications

After about 20 applications with zero interviews, reevaluate the resume, the channel, and the roles you target. Adjust one, then keep applying. In theory every resume is tailored to the job. That is unrealistic over dozens of applications, so keep [[Resume#Tailor it to the posting|one version per field]] you apply to, and adjust it for each posting. Talk to a professor who knows engineers, or meet the recruiter at a career fair, where the calls come while the reps are still on campus. The [[Getting hired#When online applications go quiet|section on when online applications go quiet]] on the Getting hired page has the stories behind the rule.

## 5. Know every line of your resume

Interviewers [go 3 to 5 layers deep](https://x.com/randomrecruiter/status/2081829972415578607) on whatever you say: how did you do that, why that way, what broke. Prepare your own projects first, then fundamentals, then coding out loud, then a handful of STAR stories. Say what you do not know, and never bluff. The [[How to interview|how to interview page]] follows one real process from the phone screen to the offer.

## 6. Match the calendar

Internship posts open before new-graduate posts, so apply in late summer and fall. Full-time hiring has two peaks, [September to November and January to March](https://ocs.yale.edu/industry-hiring-timelines/). The [[First internship|first internship page]] covers the internship calendar and what to do if a summer falls through. The [[First full-time role|first full-time role page]] covers senior-year recruiting, return offers, and reading an offer.

## 7. Keep learning after the offer

Read the datasheet, the app note, and the errata for every part you use. Take the FE exam near graduation if a PE license might ever matter to you. The [[Forever learning|forever learning page]] lists the free courses and the paperwork. The [[AI use|AI use page]] says where a tool helps in all of this and where it hurts.

## Where the rule of 20 came from

The rule of 20 came from trial and error in a real search. The [[Author's story|author's story page]] has the short version.
