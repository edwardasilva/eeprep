Build one project you can defend. Put it on a one-page resume with a measured number in every bullet you can. Ask for a referral, then apply the day the posting opens. If 20 applications bring zero interviews, change the resume or the channel before you send 20 more. Know every line of your resume when the interview comes, and never bluff. That is the whole guide. The rest of this page gives each step a few sentences and the page that goes deeper.

## 1. Build one project you can defend

Pick one build, aim it at what the company makes, and finish it with a measured number. Write down what broke and how you fixed it. One project you can explain line by line beats five you copied. [One student's projects got them a job at Apple](https://www.reddit.com/r/EngineeringStudents/comments/1odiu6l/personal_projects_that_got_you_hired_for/): an oscilloscope built on an STM32 board with 9% error, and an MCU in Vivado that ran a Mario game. [[Projects]] covers how to pick and present a build, [[Software]] covers firmware and lab scripts, and [[Hardware]] covers bench skills and builds on a student budget.

## 2. Write one page a screener can read in seconds

One column, no photo, dates at the right margin, and a result in every bullet. [One engineering hiring manager reads about 350 resumes a week](https://www.reddit.com/r/EngineeringResumes/comments/1hw10ms/12_yoe_some_long_direct_advice_in_tech_from_a/) and finds maybe 20 good ones, so the first scan has to land. [[Resume]] has the layout and the bullet pattern. [[Walkthrough]] takes Alex's page from a first draft to the finished PDF, and [[Cleanup]] is the audit to run before you send it.

## 3. Ask for a referral, then apply early

A referral is someone at the company putting your name forward for a specific job. In [Ashby's report on 38 million applications](https://www.ashbyhq.com/talent-trends-report/reports/referrals), 40% of referred candidates got an interview and 3% of people who applied on their own did. Ask before you apply. If you cannot get one, apply within two weeks of the posting, because [most recruiters read applications in the order they arrive](https://my.greenhouse.com/blogs/what-really-happens-after-you-apply-for-a-job). [[Getting hired]] lists who to ask and the message to send.

## 4. Stop after 20 quiet applications

If 20 applications bring zero interviews, the method is the problem, not the market. Change the resume or the channel before the next 20. Talk to a professor who knows engineers, or meet the recruiter at a career fair, where the calls come while the reps are still on campus. [[Getting hired#When online applications go quiet]] has the stories behind the rule.

## 5. Know every line of your resume

Interviewers [go 3 to 5 layers deep](https://x.com/randomrecruiter/status/2081829972415578607) on whatever you say: how did you do that, why that way, what broke. Prepare your own projects first, then fundamentals, then coding out loud, then a handful of STAR stories. Say what you do not know, and never bluff. [[How to interview]] follows one real process from the phone screen to the offer.

## 6. Match the calendar

Internship posts open before new-graduate posts, so apply in late summer and fall. Full-time hiring has two peaks, [September to November and January to March](https://ocs.yale.edu/industry-hiring-timelines/). [[First internship]] covers the internship calendar and what to do if a summer falls through. [[First full-time role]] covers senior-year recruiting, return offers, and reading an offer.

## 7. Keep learning after the offer

Read the datasheet, the app note, and the errata for every part you use. Take the FE exam near graduation if a PE license might ever matter to you. [[Forever learning]] lists the free courses and the paperwork. [[AI use]] says where a tool helps in all of this and where it hurts.

## My own path, in short

I started as a computer science major and had switched to electrical engineering by my third year. Before any engineering job I worked customer service, taught coding, delivered flowers, and graded calculus. A six-month electrical design co-op in 2025 taught me what I did not want, and a defense internship that summer taught me what I did. I sent hundreds of applications and found the rule of 20 by trial and error. I could not get a referral at my current employer, so I applied cold the day the posting opened. Six rounds and a take-home later, I started as an embedded software engineer in July 2026. [[About]] has the long version.
