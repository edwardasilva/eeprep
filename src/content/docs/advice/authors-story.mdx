I work full time writing embedded software for a large aerospace hardware company. This page is a short note on where the advice on the other pages comes from, so you can judge how far to trust it.

## Background

- I finished a BSEE with a CS minor in 2026.
- I was a peer mentor and co-led a first-year course for about 30 students.
- I am an IEEE member.
- As an undergraduate, I took a graduate-level elective in estimation theory and Kalman filtering.
- I taught coding to kids, 50+ students a week, and helped run STEM camps.
- I am a member of IEEE-HKN (Eta Kappa Nu).
- I volunteer with IEEE Young Professionals in Texas.

## Where the advice comes from

I began in computer science and moved to electrical engineering in my second year. Circuits and digital logic made me prefer hardware, and summer classes taken earlier meant the switch cost no extra year. Before and during school I held jobs that had nothing to do with engineering, from customer service to grading calculus for 30 to 50 students a semester. They taught me to explain a design to people who did not share my vocabulary.

A six-month design co-op on residential buildings did not fit. It showed me which roles to leave alone, and it left me with bullets that had numbers in them. The internship right after it, on real-time signal processing in C++, did fit. It pointed me at embedded software, where low-level code meets the physical side of the degree.

I spent the first semester of senior year sending hundreds of applications by hand, and I found most of the roles on LinkedIn. My early resumes were weak until I rebuilt them on a better template. The [[Resume|resume page]] has those fixes. I found the job that hired me with a job-posting scraper I wrote. Nobody at the company could refer me, so I sent a cold application the same day the posting went up, and I got through six interview rounds. The [[Getting hired#Get a referral first|section on getting a referral first]] covers the better route.

Two lessons from that search show up across these pages. The first became the rule of 50, a rule of thumb and not a measured cutoff. I started at 20. I use 50 now because at [[Quick setup#Where the rule of 50 came from|a 3 percent interview rate]], a run of 20 applications with zero interviews happens about half the time by luck alone. The [[Getting hired#When online applications go quiet|section on when online applications go quiet]] says what to check when you reach 50.

The second lesson is that an unfinished project still counts when the progress is real. An unfinished solar tracker was part of what got me the signal processing internship. The [[Projects|projects page]] covers how to pick a project and present it. Its [[Projects#Unfinished and stalled projects|section on unfinished projects]] covers how to list one that is not done.

One more habit is worth keeping: never bluff in an interview. The [[How to interview|how to interview page]] follows the process step by step.
